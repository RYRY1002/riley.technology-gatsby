Way back in 2017, YouTube recommended to me a video that would go on to change my life for the foreseeable future. That video was [MiningGodBruce](https://www.youtube.com/MiningGodBruce)'s [Subtlety Minecraft music video](https://youtu.be/C1ZN_NRBWwI?feature=shared "TOPAZ - Subtlety | Minecraft Music Video").

Most viewers enjoyed this video, albeit more than most, but in my case, it utterly captivated me.

My Autism diagnosis probably had a large part to play in that, after all the video activated my sensory responses (ie, [hand flapping](https://certifiedautismcenter.com/2021/10/12/autism-stimming-and-hand-flapping-what-are-the-key-causes-and-behaviors/)) like no other.

The classic response to an amazing piece of art from any 10-year-old? Try and recreate it. So that's what I set out to do. Fortunately, Bruce had included a vast swath of details about the video's creation, even including his project files and source code.

---

Skip ahead to mid-2020. I came into possession of a computer that would allow me to work on this project. I had spent the last few years developing my programming skills in my spare time on [Scratch](https://scratch.mit.edu/) and as an extracurricular activity at my school where I learnt [Processing](https://processing.org/). Using a few old tutorials on developing shaders from the folks at [Continuum](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLTCOf8PYOLclR7kLOuh489VD4ynMM1RIv), and [Past Life Pro](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLLbNWy4Fb15Y-b0-pELRQxQObjnUl1DjI), I started working away at the shader.

Bruce had been fortunate enough to provide the source code for the Minecraft shader he used to make his videos, and I used that as a jumping off point to start work on my project (and learn GLSL at the same time). Like any curious programmer working in an unfamiliar codebase, I started tinkering with the code, disabling certain lines and changing variable values to figure out what purpose they served.

During my time working on this project, I took [hundreds of screenshots](https://photos.app.goo.gl/wYMRYWAyX56PUq588) of interesting errors, progress milestones, and images of things to get feedback on. They'll continue to be interspersed throughout this article.

I started off by generating the terrain that would be used in the video. Due to a few quirks resulting from changes to Minecraft's terrain generation throughout the years, I ended up using an [ancient mod](https://www.minecraftforum.net/forums/mapping-and-modding-java-edition/minecraft-mods/1280998-1-3-2-generator-ports-generate-terrain-from-older) for Minecraft 1.2.5 (released in April 2012, yes really). This process consists of finding a random world seed that looks nice and then moving the camera forward for thousands of blocks to get the game to generate more terrain. The amount of terrain that needs to be generated was calculated in advance.

<AllAccessPass title="Terrain length formula" readMore="See the formula">

We can calculate the amount of terrain needed, but first we need to figure out how much we are going to speed the video up.

The first section of my video (I felt like butter in the heat), lasts for 57 seconds and 3606 blocks. In Minecraft, the player walks at 4.617 blocks/second. Therefore,

$$
\frac{3606}{4.617}=781.0266...\text{ seconds}
\\
\frac{781.0266}{57}=13.7022...\text{x speedup}
$$

So, when we calculate how many blocks of terrain we need to generate, we can do

$$
13.7022...\times4.617=63.2631...
\\
\text{Seconds required}\times63.2631...=\text{Length of terrain needed}
$$

As an example, let's apply this formula to the second section of the video (time travels in my mind), which is 82 seconds long

$$
\begin{array}{rcl}
82\times63.2631...&=&5187.5789\text{ blocks}
\\
&=&5188\text{ blocks (rounded)}
\end{array}
$$

For anyone using this as a guide to create their own Acid Interstate video, the full constant is $63.263157894736842105263157894737$.

</AllAccessPass>

The terrain is then converted into a newer version of the game (1.12.2, released September 2017) where I do the rest of the work.

The terrain is cleaned up, moved to the world origin, organised and rails placed in [MCEdit](https://github.com/Podshot/MCEdit-Unified).

The next task was to place the torches that would be in-sync with the music. To do this, I quickly put together a pipeline in Adobe Premiere Pro with a counter from the starting X ordinate of the section to the ending X ordinate, sped up by the amount that the final video would be sped up (see above). I then manually placed a large white box on the frame that I wanted the torch to pass the camera. After that, I could play back the video, and find the appropriate coordinate for each torch, which I then manually placed in Minecraft.

This whole process of generating terrain, moving it around, and placing torches took about 2 weeks.

---

From here, I spent the better part of the next 6 months of my free time developing the shader that would be used in the video. I don't really have much to share from this process, I was simultaneously learning the basics of Git and GLSL at the same time, and unfortunately my 14-year-old self didn't much care to take down notes of what I was doing. From what I recall, it was mostly a process of tinkering around with variables to get things to a vague idea of how I wanted things to be in my mind.

The shader acts more like a script than a shader. There are a series of X ordinates in the shader that determine where something should happen, such as the terrain moving up/down during a particularly heavy beat, where a portal should be, and when the time cycle should move forward/backward.

The most complicated part of the shader are the portals which transition between one section of the video and another. These are accomplished entirely within the shader.

Both parts of the terrain, from the previous section and from the next, are placed next to each other in the world. Both pieces of terrain are then moved to the same point in space with a vertex shader, and then a fragment shader decides which piece of terrain will be visible via a second pseudo-frustrum constructed out of the cobblestone gateways.

Because both sides of the terrain need to fit inside of a limited render distance, the width of the models is shrunk to half the render distance to fit. This is covered up by ramping up the fog to cover the render distance border.

As for the terrain deformations, they are achieved with a vertex shader that displaces the verticies of each block according to an function which uses the X ordinate as a constant.

A lot of the fun I had when working on this project was finding novel deformation functions to use. This involved tinkering with variables, moving sin and cos functions around, messing with angles and position swizzle masks until things looked interesting and unique.
